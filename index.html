    Quick question before you start

    Someone offers you £100. You can take it today, or take exactly £100 one year from now. Which do you choose?

    Chapter 1

    Money now is worth more than money later

    This idea is called the time value of money, and the whole of DCF is built on it. There are three reasons £100 today beats £100 next year:

    InflationPrices rise, so £100 buys less next year than it does today.
    Opportunity costMoney you have now can be saved or invested to earn interest.
    RiskA promise of future money might not be kept. Cash in hand is certain.

    To compare future money with money today, we discount it. The result is called the present value (PV):

    PV = Future amount ÷ (1 + r)n   r = discount rate, n = number of years

    Chapter 2

    Profit is not the same as cash

    A DCF values the free cash flow (FCF) a business produces: the cash left over after it has paid its running costs and tax and invested in its future. That cash could be paid out to the people who fund the business.

      Chapter 3

      Choosing the discount rate

      The discount rate is the return investors expect for putting their money into this business. The riskier the cash flows, the higher the return investors demand, so the higher the discount rate and the lower the value today.

      Calculation

      A bit more technical: WACC

      Companies are funded by two groups. Shareholders (equity) and lenders (debt). Each expects a return, so analysts blend the two into the weighted average cost of capital, or WACC. That blended figure is usually the discount rate in a DCF.

      The cost of equity is often estimated with CAPM: a safe return plus extra for risk. Beta measures how much a share price moves compared with the whole stock market. A beta of 1.5 means it tends to move 50% more than the market. Debt is cheaper partly because interest is paid before tax, which saves the company tax.

      Risk-free rate 4% and market risk premium 5% are fixed here.
      Interest rate on debt 6%, tax rate 25%.
      WACC (discount rate)

      In your own model you can simply choose a rate. 8% to 12% is a sensible range for most established companies.

      Chapter 4

      The terminal value: everything after year 5

      Businesses do not stop after five years, but forecasting each year to 2080 would be guesswork. Instead, analysts forecast about five years in detail, then assume cash flow grows at a small, steady rate forever. The value of all those later years, squeezed into one number, is the terminal value (TV).

      TV = FCF5 × (1 + g) ÷ (r − g)   g = long-run growth rate

      This is the growing perpetuity formula. It only works if g is smaller than r. Long-run growth is usually set at 2% to 3%, close to how fast the whole economy grows, because no company can outgrow the economy forever.

      Terminal value at year 5
      g is not below r, so the formula breaks. It would say the business is worth an infinite amount, because cash grows faster than you discount it. Lower g or raise r.

      Dashed outlines show the cash each year (£m). Filled bars show what that cash is worth today. Because each later year is discounted more, the filled bars fade towards zero, which is why "forever" still adds up to a finite number.
